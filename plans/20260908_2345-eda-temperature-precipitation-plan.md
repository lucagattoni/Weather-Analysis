## 3. What reconnaissance established

Run before planning, on the full 706,369-row file. These are facts, not
assumptions, and they drive most of the design below.

**The series is structurally perfect.** 1946-01-01 00:00 to 2026-08-01 00:00 UTC,
706,369 hourly rows, zero duplicate timestamps, zero missing timestamps against a
complete hourly range. Only 306 NaN cells in the whole file (255 `vis`, 24 `clht`,
24 `clamt`, one each in `vappr`, `rhum`, `wddir`). 2026 is partial: 5,089 hours,
ending 1 August.

**An undocumented indicator block covers 40% of the record.** All five `ind`
columns simultaneously hold the value `111` for 286,967 rows, in one contiguous
run from 1993-09-01 01:00 to 2026-05-28 00:00 (with a single one-hour gap at
2026-05-21 21:00). Met Éireann's own `KeyHourly.txt` documents codes 0–7 only and
never mentions 111. The natural reading is that it marks the automated
observation era, but that is an inference and the plan will label it as one.

**The trace-precipitation flags vanish at that same boundary, and this is the
single most consequential finding for doc 3.** `KeyHourly.txt` defines rain
indicator 2 as "trace or sum of precipitation" and 3 as "trace or sum of
deposition". Their share of all hours by decade:

| Decade | 1940s | 1950s | 1960s | 1970s | 1980s | 1990s | 2000s | 2010s | 2020s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share of hours flagged 2 or 3 | 23.7% | 27.5% | 31.0% | 27.7% | 28.2% | 9.8% | 0.0% | 0.0% | 0.3% |

Those rows are 97.6% and 99.96% zero-valued respectively. So in the manual era
roughly a quarter to a third of every hour was an explicit trace observation
recorded as 0.0 mm, and in the automated era trace is not reported at all.
Consequence: **any statistic counting rain hours, dry spells or wet-hour
frequency is not comparable across September 1993.** Annual totals are far less
affected — 749.3 mm mean for 1946–1992 against 775.7 mm for 1994–2025 — so doc 3
is built on daily and annual totals, not hourly occurrence.

**Zeros that are probably outages.** Sunshine duration reads exactly 0.0 for 283
consecutive hours from 2025-02-07, spanning about twelve February days of
daylight. That is an instrument gap stored as zero. Rainfall's longest zero run is
717 hours from 1955-07-03, which is plausible given that summer's Irish drought.
Doc 1 will carry an explicit rule and a detected-outage table rather than treat
every zero as an observation.

**The temperature sign flag is confirmed and redundant.** `ind_temp == 1` means
"negative" per the key file, and 4,021 of its 4,022 rows do have `temp < 0`. The
CSV already carries the minus sign, so the flag adds nothing and is not used.

**Sentinels, consistent with the existing chunking script.** `clht` 999 = no
ceiling (26.5% of rows), `clamt` 9 = sky obscured (one row), `wddir` 0 = calm, not
north (1.77%).

## 4. Method and its known weaknesses

Each trend is reported three ways because the disagreements are informative.

- **OLS slope** on the annual series, quoted per decade with a confidence
  interval. Weakness: annual weather series are autocorrelated, so a naive OLS
  interval is too narrow. The interval will be widened for lag-1 autocorrelation
  and the adjustment stated, not hidden.
- **Mann-Kendall test with Sen's slope**, non-parametric, robust to the skew and
  heavy tails that make OLS unreliable on rainfall. Weakness: it tests for
  monotonic trend, so it under-reports a trend that reverses.
- **LOESS smoother** for shape, to show whether a straight line is even the right
  description. Weakness: the span is a choice; it will be stated and one
  alternative span shown.

Where all three agree the finding is reported plainly. Where they disagree the
disagreement is the finding.

**The 1993 break is tested, not assumed.** Both docs 2 and 3 will check whether
the anomaly series shows a step at September 1993 by comparing the trend fitted
to 1946–1992 alone, 1994–2025 alone, and the whole record. If the sub-period
slopes bracket the full-record slope the break is not driving the result; if they
do not, every trend number in the doc is qualified accordingly. This is the main
threat to the headline claim and it gets its own section, not a footnote.

## 5. Code layout

Following the project rule that Python is a build-time step in `scripts/` run by
`uv run` with a pinned sibling `.lock`.

```
scripts/eda_common.py        loading, cleaning, aggregation ladder, trend fitting
scripts/eda_report.py        --section {review,temperature,precipitation,all}
scripts/eda_report.py.lock   pinned: pandas, numpy, matplotlib, scipy, statsmodels
EDA/01-data-review.md        narrative, embeds figures and tables
EDA/02-temperature.md
EDA/03-precipitation.md
EDA/figures/*.png            every chart, regenerated deterministically
EDA/stats/*.csv              every number the docs quote, machine-readable
```

One script with a `--section` flag rather than three scripts, so there is one
dependency set and one lock to keep current.

**How the docs stay honest.** The script writes every figure and every quoted
number into `EDA/figures/` and `EDA/stats/`. The prose is written by hand around
them, and each doc names the exact command that regenerates its inputs. A re-run
that changes a number then shows up as a git diff in `EDA/stats/`, so drift
between the prose and the data is visible rather than silent. The alternative
considered and rejected was generating the prose from the script too: it removes
drift entirely but makes the narrative painful to edit, which is the wrong
trade for a document meant to be read.

Determinism: no timestamps in output, fixed figure size and DPI, sorted
iteration order, matplotlib `Agg` backend. Same input, byte-identical output, so
a re-run does not churn git.

## 6. Doc 1 — complete data review

1. Provenance: station, coordinates, height, period, licence, source file.
2. Schema: all 21 raw columns mapped to 13 variables, 5 indicators, 2 SYNOP codes,
   with the duplicate `ind` header names resolved.
3. Completeness: timestamp continuity, duplicates, NaN census per column, rows per
   year, and a coverage figure.
4. Indicator flags: the documented codes from `KeyHourly.txt`, the 111 block, the
   trace-flag collapse, each with the figure that shows it.
5. Sentinels and zeros-as-missing: the rule adopted, and the table of detected
   suspect runs per variable.
6. Univariate summary per variable: count, missing, min, max, mean, median, the
   5th/25th/75th/95th percentiles, skew, and a histogram.
7. Seasonality: monthly climatology per variable.
8. Diurnal cycle: hour-of-day profile per variable, which also exposes any
   variable whose reporting hours changed.
9. Correlation matrix, with the physically redundant pairs called out —
   `temp`/`wetb`/`dewpt`/`vappr`/`rhum` are five views of two quantities.
10. **Data-quality register**: one table, every issue found, its extent, and which
    downstream analyses it invalidates. This is the part docs 2 and 3 cite.

## 7. Doc 2 — temperature

Built on daily mean, daily minimum and daily maximum derived from the hourly
series, then monthly, seasonal and annual.

1. The annual mean series, with 1961–1990 anomalies.
2. Trend: OLS, Mann-Kendall/Sen and LOESS, on the annual mean, quoted per decade.
3. Trend by season, because Irish winters and summers are not expected to warm at
   the same rate; four panels.
4. Decadal table: mean, anomaly against 1961–1990, warmest and coldest year.
5. Distribution shift: daily-mean histograms by 30-year period, to separate a
   shift of the whole distribution from a widening of its tails.
6. Threshold counts per year: frost days (Tmin < 0), ice days (Tmax < 0), warm
   days (Tmax ≥ 20), summer days (Tmax ≥ 25).
7. Diurnal temperature range and its trend, a recognised fingerprint that behaves
   differently from the mean.
8. Anomaly register: the ten warmest and coldest months and years, ranked.
9. The 1993 break test from §4, and the caveats that survive it.

Note on daily minimum and maximum: these are computed from hourly readings, so
they are hourly extremes, not the true daily extremes a max/min thermometer
records. The difference is small but systematic, and it will be stated wherever
threshold counts are quoted.

## 8. Doc 3 — precipitation

Opens with the trace-flag finding from §3, because it determines what the rest of
the document is allowed to say.

1. Why the analysis uses daily and annual totals rather than hourly occurrence.
2. Annual totals with 1961–1990 anomalies, and the trend three ways.
3. Seasonal totals and their trends: whether the annual figure hides a
   redistribution between seasons.
4. Monthly climatology, compared between 1961–1990 and 1991–2020.
5. Wet-day statistics on daily totals at the 0.2, 1 and 10 mm thresholds, with the
   pre/post-1993 comparability caveat applied to each.
6. Intensity: maximum hourly and daily falls per year, and the R95p and R99p
   percentile indices, which measure whether rain is arriving in heavier bursts.
7. Dry and wet spell lengths on daily data, with the same caveat.
8. Decadal table and an extremes register: wettest days, wettest and driest
   months, wettest and driest years.
9. Caveats, including the ones that make some of the above weaker than it looks.

## 9. Out of scope

Not built unless asked: any change to the TypeScript app or `public/data/`;

## 10. Risks

| Risk | Handling |
|---|---|
| The 111 block is a real instrument change that biases trends | Tested explicitly in §4; every headline number qualified by the result |
| Trace-flag collapse silently corrupts rainfall frequency stats | Doc 3 is built on totals; frequency stats carry the caveat inline, not in a footnote |
| Hourly-derived daily min/max differ from thermometer extremes | Stated wherever thresholds are quoted |
| Autocorrelation makes trends look more significant than they are | OLS intervals widened for lag-1 autocorrelation, adjustment stated |
| 2026 is a partial year | Excluded from all annual statistics and trends; stated in each doc |
